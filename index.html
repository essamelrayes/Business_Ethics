Foundations of business ethics

Start here: the vocabulary everything else builds on.

Key definitions

TermDefinition
MoralityNorms, values and beliefs defining right and wrong for an individual or community.
EthicsThe study of morality; systematic rules for determining right and wrong. From the Greek ethos, meaning custom or character.
Business ethicsMoral principles guiding behavior in the business world.

Ethics vs. morals

EthicsMorals
Guiding principles of conductPrinciples for judgments of right and wrong
Influenced by profession or organizationInfluenced by society, culture and religion
Related to professional workNot related to professional work
Uniform compared to moralsVary across cultures

Why people don't act ethically

  1. Conflict between ethics and interests
  2. Management pressure
  3. Self-interest to achieve objectives
  4. Intensive competition pressures
  5. Toxic organizational culture
  6. Absence of leading by example
  7. No ethics training

How to commit to ethics

  1. Comply with laws, which are driven from ethics and morals
  2. Fulfill promises and agreements
  3. Be transparent: disclose, don't hide problems

Sources of ethics

  • Religion: moral framework, guiding principles
  • Laws: protect ethical behavior
  • Culture and society: norms and values

Explore each theory

Utilitarianism

Bentham, Mill

  • Principle: the right action is the greatest good for the largest number.
  • Application: cost-benefit analysis, stakeholder impact.
  • Strengths: common in business; considers everyone affected.
  • Weaknesses: can't predict the future; ignores justice and individual rights.
  • Trolley problem: sacrifice one to save many?

Egoism

Self-interest is the moral principle

  • Businesses may use poor materials to maximize profit.
  • Example: layoffs to protect shareholder value.

Hedonism

Pleasure is the highest good

  • Egotistical: only your own pleasure matters.
  • Altruistic: pleasure for all people.
  • Criticism: ignores freedom and fairness.

Deontological theory

Kant

  • Duty-based: morality is determined by adherence to rules.
  • Non-consequentialist: the act itself matters, not the outcome.
  • All humans are equal regardless of gender, religion or health.
  • Example: an employee refuses to access a confidential file.

Virtue theory

Aristotle

  • Focuses on character, not rules or consequences.
  • Virtues: honesty, courage, generosity, truthfulness.
  • Acquired through practice and habit.
  • Goal: a flourishing life, not just profit.

Relativism

Ethics depends on culture, society or personal feelings

  • The same action may be right in one society and wrong in another.
  • Example: child labor criticism. A relativist says it is just opinion.

CSR: concept and evolution

Definition. CSR is a voluntary commitment to address social, environmental and ethical issues beyond legal obligations.

Key elements

  1. Social initiatives (philanthropy, community)
  2. Environmental stewardship
  3. Ethical business practices
  4. Stakeholder engagement

Evolution timeline

  1. 19th centuryEarly philanthropy (Carnegie, Rockefeller)
  2. Early 20th centuryLabor movements, worker rights
  3. Post-WWIIMultinational power, academic interest
  4. 1960s–70sEthical awareness, scandals
  5. 1980s–90sCSR as a distinct practice, stakeholder theory
  6. 2000s+Sustainability, climate change
  7. PresentTechnology, data privacy, AI ethics

Shift: obligation to strategy

TraditionalModern
"Do good to look good"Strategic alignment with business goals
Fixed annual budgetLong-term relationships
Spread across many organizationsFocused partnerships
Avoid controversial issuesAddress core business issues

Six corporate social initiatives

Exams love asking you to tell these apart. The key distinction is in the first table.

InitiativeDescriptionKey distinction
1. Cause promotionsFunds or resources to increase awarenessPromotional focus, not tied to sales
2. Cause-related marketingPercentage of revenue to a cause, based on salesTied to product sales
3. Corporate social marketingBehavior change campaignsFocus on behavior change
4. Corporate philanthropyDirect contributions (cash, grants, in-kind)Most traditional
5. Community volunteeringEmployees volunteer timePersonal involvement
6. Socially responsible business practicesDiscretionary practices supporting causesInternal operations focus

1. Cause promotions

What: funds or in-kind contributions to increase awareness. Persuasive communications, awareness building.

Examples: Body Shop animal testing ban, Ramadan charity campaigns.

BenefitsBrand positioning, customer loyalty, partnerships
ConcernsVisibility can get lost; hard to track ROI

2. Cause-related marketing (CRM)

What: contribution tied to product sales. Mutually beneficial: funds for the charity, sales for the company.

Examples: WaMoola For Schools, RED Sony laptop.

Types: specified amount per product, percentage of sales, matching contributions.

BenefitsNew customers, increased sales, brand identity
ConcernsContract complexity, consumer skepticism, tracking systems

3. Corporate social marketing

What: behavior change campaigns (health, safety, environment). Focus on changing individual behavior.

Examples: tobacco prevention, Subway healthy eating, litter prevention.

BenefitsBrand positioning, profitability through cost reduction
ConcernsConsumer skepticism, requires expertise, slow results

4. Corporate philanthropy Most traditional

What: direct contributions: cash, grants, scholarships, products, services.

Examples: United Way, YMCA, disaster relief.

BenefitsReputation, community goodwill, brand position
ConcernsLess visibility, measurement challenges

5. Community volunteering

What: employees donate time and talents.

Types: community projects, health and safety, environmental.

BenefitsCommunity relationships, employee satisfaction, corporate image
ConcernsExpensive, dispersed impact, tracking difficulty

6. Socially responsible business practices

What: discretionary practices and investments supporting social causes. Not mandated by law; internal operations focus.

Examples: environmental design, ethical sourcing, employee well-being.

BenefitsCost reduction, brand preference, employee satisfaction
ConcernsSkepticism about motives, long-term commitment questions

CSR benefits and challenges

Benefits of CSR

InvestorsIncreased appeal, stock value

Major challenges

  1. Choosing the social issue: fit with the business, stakeholder opinions
  2. Selecting the initiative: visibility, cost, alignment
  3. Measurement: ROI is difficult to track
  4. Resource allocation: balance profit vs. CSR
  5. Maintaining consistency: avoid superficial efforts

Keys to success

  1. Choose a few issues with synergy to the mission
  2. Focus on issues relevant to the communities where you operate
  3. Support them long-term
  4. Form cross-functional teams
  5. Get senior management buy-in
  6. Establish clear, measurable goals
  7. Develop communication plans

Exam tips

  1. Know the theories. Be able to apply utilitarianism, deontology and virtue theory to scenarios.
  2. Distinguish initiatives. Cause promotion vs. CRM vs. social marketing.
  3. Remember examples. Match companies to their CSR initiatives.
  4. Benefits vs. concerns. Be able to list both for each initiative.
  5. Evolution. Understand the shift from obligation to strategy.
  6. Case application. Practice analyzing ethical dilemmas with the frameworks.

Self-test

Answer in your head first, then open the card.

How do cause promotion and cause-related marketing differ?
Cause promotion builds awareness and isn't tied to sales. Cause-related marketing gives a percentage or set amount per sale, so the charity and the company both benefit.
Name one strength and one weakness of utilitarianism.
Strength: it's common in business and considers everyone affected. Weakness: it can't predict the future and ignores justice and individual rights.
Kant or Aristotle: who focuses on duty, and who on character?
Kant's deontology is duty-based: the act matters, not the outcome. Aristotle's virtue theory focuses on character and a flourishing life.
How do ethics differ from morals?
Ethics are guiding principles shaped by profession or organization and tied to work, and they're more uniform. Morals are shaped by society, culture and religion, and vary across cultures.
What is the shift in CSR from "obligation" to "strategy"?
From "do good to look good" with a fixed budget spread across many organizations, to alignment with business goals, long-term relationships, focused partnerships and core business issues.
Which initiative is the most traditional, and which focuses on behavior change?
Corporate philanthropy is the most traditional. Corporate social marketing targets behavior change.
What are the three ways to commit to ethics?
Comply with laws, fulfill promises and agreements, and be transparent by disclosing problems.
Define triple bottom line and greenwashing.
Triple bottom line: people, planet, profit. Greenwashing: false claims of environmental responsibility.
